Page 8 — Hearing

Support by sense

Hearing

Hearing Low

Open-plan noise, notifications, and overlapping talk can make concentration harder than the work itself.

Before the workday

  1. Keep a quiet buffer before the first meeting, so the day does not start already full of sound.
  2. On a noisy commute, use noise-reducing headphones or leave a little earlier on a quieter route.
  3. Look at the calendar the night before and place ten quiet minutes on either side of the noisiest meeting.

During the workday

  1. Use a quieter room, a booked focus space, or a home block for work that cannot compete with the floor.
  2. Treat noise-reducing headphones as a normal tool, and protect one or two low-interruption focus blocks.
  3. Silence non-urgent alerts in those blocks, and step out of overlapping conversations instead of tracking all of them.

After work

  1. Take the commute down: less audio, or none, so office sound is not still being processed on the way home.
  2. Give the first part of the evening a low-conversation window before more talking or household noise.
  3. After a meeting-heavy day, a short walk without headphones often clears leftover noise better than another busy room.
“

A quieter start gives the nervous system room to settle before the first meeting.

Page 9 — Touch

Support by sense

Touch

Touch Low

Crowded desks, scratchy seating, and unexpected proximity can nag at attention through the day.

Before the workday

  1. Choose clothing and layers that stay comfortable for a full day, and pack a soft spare layer.
  2. If the commute is crowded, leave slightly earlier or later, or stand at the edge to reduce unexpected contact.
  3. Agree the desk setup before the day starts: an edge seat, a clear boundary, and no shared chair-back.

During the workday

  1. Use seating with more personal space — end of a row, edge of the open plan, or a less crowded zone.
  2. Choose a less packed meeting layout when one is available, and take a short break when proximity builds.
  3. Treat clothing or chair discomfort early: change layer, posture, or seat before it becomes the whole afternoon.

After work

  1. Change into comfortable clothes soon after finishing, before more contact is expected.
  2. Use a warm shower or a quiet room as a reset after a day of being brushed past.
  3. Ask for a little space at home first — conversation can wait until the body is no longer on guard.
“

Clear agreements about space and contact make a shared office easier to stay in.

Page 10 — Movement

Support by sense

Movement

Movement Medium

Some days sitting is easy. On others the body needs a change of pace before focus comes back.

Before the workday

  1. Notice whether the morning feels restless or already tired, and match the commute — a little more walk, or a calmer one.
  2. Keep two or three movements under three minutes in mind before the day starts.
  3. Leave a few spare minutes on arrival so the first task does not begin in a rush.

During the workday

  1. Take a short movement break when restlessness shows up, rather than waiting until focus has already dropped.
  2. Change posture across the day — sit, stand, a brief walk — instead of one unbroken seated block.
  3. Put a small buffer between back-to-back meetings so the body can arrive before the next demand.

After work

  1. If the day was too still, use a short walk as the check-in before the evening routine.
  2. If the day was rushed, slow down first: a steady walk, a stretch, or both feet on the floor.
  3. Let that reset finish before errands or plans, so the pace of work is not carried straight into the night.
“

Match the movement to the morning: a short walk when the body is restless, a slower settle when the day was already rushed.

Page 11 — Vision

Support by sense

Vision

Vision High

A plain desk and long dense documents can feel flat, so attention goes looking for colour, movement, or a view.

Before the workday

  1. Give the eyes something to lock onto before work: outdoor light on the commute, or a quick look at a visual checklist.
  2. Set up a colour-coded list or a visual timer at the desk as you arrive.
  3. Decide which block of the day needs the richest visual setup, and which can stay simple.

During the workday

  1. Use colour-coded planners, timers, or checklists so attention has something clear to hold.
  2. Keep a plant, a window, or a short look-far break after dense reading.
  3. Place the hardest visual work in the more alert part of the day, not in the flat mid-afternoon stretch.

After work

  1. Change the scene: outdoor light or a different room before more reading at home.
  2. Add a little colour or movement to the evening if the desk day was visually plain.
  3. Keep the first screen at home optional, so the eyes are not asked for another dense block straight away.
“

Colour, a view, and something for the eyes to hold keep a plain desk from going flat.

Page 12 — Smell and taste

Support by sense

Smell and taste

Smell and taste Medium

Office scents and strong lunch flavours are usually manageable, and become intrusive on a tired or deadline-heavy day.

Before the workday

  1. Pack both a milder lunch and a more interesting one, so flavour can match how loaded the day already feels.
  2. If yesterday’s scent load lingered, choose milder products before leaving home.
  3. Notice the commute — food courts, perfume, fuel — and plan a short outdoor pause before the building if it was strong.

During the workday

  1. Keep the option to move to fresher air or a milder lunch when smell starts to intrude.
  2. Treat an early cue — a headache, nausea, or drifting focus — as a reason to change seat or scent, not to push on.
  3. Use lunch as a reset: familiar flavour on a heavy day, a little more interest on a flat one.

After work

  1. Take the commute in fresher air after a strong-smelling office.
  2. Keep the first meal at home familiar if the day was full of scent, or a bit more flavourful if it was bland.
  3. Open a window before stronger cooking smells, so home does not stack on top of the office.
“

Flavour and scent can follow the day: milder when the load is already high, a little more interest when the day feels flat.

Page 13 — Everyday load

Support by sense

Everyday load

Everyday load Low

It is often the pile-up — noise, light, people, and pace together — that leaves little left for the commute or for home.

Before the workday

  1. Keep the morning routine calmer, and start the hardest work while capacity is still highest.
  2. Look at the stacked demands — meetings, open plan, commute — and decide in advance where one quieter buffer will sit.
  3. Name one thing that can wait until tomorrow, so the day is not planned at full capacity before it begins.

During the workday

  1. Put demanding thinking earlier, and keep lighter tasks for later in the day.
  2. Protect lunch away from the desk, in a quieter place, before overload has already arrived.
  3. Take a short sensory break when the pile-up starts — five quieter minutes — rather than at the end of the day.

After work

  1. Decompress before the next demand: a quieter commute, a pause in the car, or a short buffer at the door.
  2. Give home a low-input first stretch before conversation, chores, or more screens.
  3. Treat recovery as part of the work plan, not whatever is left if the day allows.
“

Recovery after work is part of the plan, not whatever is left if the day allows.

Page 15 — At work

At work

Where the senses meet the workday

The sensory bucket

Every meeting, notification, and crowded walk adds to the load. When the bucket is full, focus, patience, and recovery all get harder — even if the tasks themselves are familiar.

What helps this pattern

  • A quieter block for the hardest thinking.
  • Space around the desk, and fewer back-to-back meetings.
  • A planned decompress after the noisy parts of the day.
